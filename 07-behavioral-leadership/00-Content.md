# Behavioral & Leadership: Learning & Interview Roadmap

For a mobile engineer moving toward senior, lead, and architect roles. Behavioral rounds test **how you work with people, make decisions, and handle problems**. The best answers are real stories, told clearly, with a result.
This folder has **its own numbering, 1 to 21**. Each topic gets its own doc later.

**How to read references:** a plain number (`7`) is a topic in this folder. `ARCH`, `SD`, `MOB`, `BE`, and `AI` are the numbered roadmaps in `09-architecture`, `05-system-design`, `06-mobile-engineering`, `04-backend-engineering`, and `08-artificial-intelligence`.

**How this fits with the other folders:** the technical folders teach what to know. This folder teaches **how to tell the story of what you did**. Good stories usually come from work covered elsewhere, for example architecture decisions (`ARCH 3`, `ARCH 16`), incidents and releases (`SD 27`, `SD 28`), and mobile quality work (`MOB 11`, `MOB 15`).

# What to Follow Before Any Behavioral Interview

*The same eight steps work for any company and any level. Write each output down.*

| Step | What to do | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Know the target | Read the job description, the level, and the company's stated values | A list of the 5 to 6 competencies this role tests |
| 2. Collect your stories | List your real projects, decisions, incidents, disagreements, and mistakes from the last 3 to 5 years | A raw list of 10 to 15 events |
| 3. Shape each story | Write each in STAR form with numbers and your own actions | 8 to 10 stories of about two minutes each |
| 4. Map stories to competencies | Match each story to the questions it can answer | A story matrix (topic 3) |
| 5. Add the lessons | For each story, write what you learned and what you would do differently | One lesson line per story |
| 6. Practise out loud | Tell each story with a timer, and record yourself | Clear, short, natural versions |
| 7. Prepare your questions | Write 5 good questions to ask each interviewer | A question list (topic 19) |
| 8. Prepare the basics | Your career story, why this company, and why now (topic 16) | Three short answers |

**In the room:** listen to the question, pause for a moment, choose a story, tell it in STAR order, and finish with the result and the lesson.

**Priority order when time is short:** steps 2, 3, and 6. A few well-told real stories beat many vague ones.

# Stage 1: Foundations

## 1. How Behavioral Interviews Work

- 🟢 What interviewers look for: signals for ownership, communication, collaboration, judgment, learning, and impact
- 🟢 Past behaviour predicts future behaviour, so they ask for real examples
- 🟢 Common formats: "Tell me about a time...", follow-up probes, and situational "What would you do...?"
- 🟢 Level expectations: mid (you do the work well), senior (you own outcomes and help others), staff or architect (you shape direction across teams)
- 🟢 Interviewers take notes and score against a rubric. Give clear evidence
- 🟢 Be honest. Stories that are invented fall apart under follow-up questions
- 🟡 Who interviews you: hiring manager, peers, cross-functional partners, and sometimes a dedicated behavioral interviewer
- 🟡 How the answers are compared across candidates (calibration)
- 🟡 Red flags: blaming others, no ownership, no results, claiming credit for the team, and negativity about past employers
- 🟡 Remote interview basics: camera, sound, and pacing

## 2. The STAR Method

- 🟢 **S**ituation: the context, in one or two sentences
- 🟢 **T**ask: your goal or responsibility
- 🟢 **A**ction: what **you** did, step by step. This is the longest part
- 🟢 **R**esult: the outcome, with numbers when you can
- 🟢 Add a short lesson at the end (STAR plus learning)
- 🟢 Say "I" for your actions, and "we" for team context
- 🟢 Aim for about two minutes, and keep the situation short
- 🟢 Quantify the result: time saved, crash rate, users, revenue, cost, quality
- 🟡 Variations: CAR (context, action, result) and SOAR
- 🟡 Adapt the same story to different questions by changing the emphasis
- 🟡 Common mistakes: too much background, too little action, no result, and rambling
- 🟡 Recovering when you lose the thread: pause and restate

## 3. Building Your Story Bank

- 🟢 Collect events from real work: projects, launches, incidents, decisions, disagreements, mistakes, mentoring
- 🟢 Prepare 8 to 10 stories that each cover several competencies
- 🟢 A story matrix: rows are stories, columns are competencies, and each cell shows how well it fits
- 🟢 For each story, capture: context, stakes, your role, the options, your actions, the numbers, and the lesson
- 🟢 Keep at least one story for each: a big win, a failure, a conflict, a decision, an incident, and a time you helped someone grow
- 🟡 Keep a "short version" (30 seconds) and a "full version" (2 minutes) of each
- 🟡 Choose stories at the right level of scope for the role you want
- 🟡 Keep a running log of achievements, so you never have to remember from scratch
- 🟡 Protect confidential details: describe the situation without naming private data

# Stage 2: Core Stories

## 4. Project Challenges & Impact

- 🟢 Pick a project where the problem was hard, and say why
- 🟢 Show the challenge: ambiguity, a tight deadline, changing scope, unclear ownership, or a technical limit
- 🟢 Show your role and decisions, not only the team's result
- 🟢 Show the impact with numbers: crash-free rate, startup time, conversion, retention, cost, delivery time
- 🟢 Explain how you measured success
- 🟡 Mobile examples: an offline-first rebuild, a slow startup fix, a checkout redesign, a large migration (Java to Kotlin, XML to Compose, RxJava to coroutines)
- 🟡 Trade-offs you accepted and what you deferred
- 🟡 What you would do differently
- 🟡 Common mistake: describing the project, not your part in it

## 5. Technical Decisions

- 🟢 Tell the story of a real decision: the problem, the options, how you compared them, the choice, and the outcome
- 🟢 Show the criteria: cost, risk, time, team skills, maintainability, user impact
- 🟢 Show how you got evidence: a prototype, a benchmark, or data
- 🟢 Show how you brought others along, and how you handled disagreement
- 🟢 Be ready for "what if you had chosen the other option?"
- 🟡 A decision you reversed, and why you had the courage to
- 🟡 A decision made with little information, and how you reduced the risk
- 🟡 Recording the decision (ADR, design doc) (`ARCH 3`, `ARCH 14`)
- 🟡 Build vs buy, library choice, and framework choice stories

## 6. Architecture Decisions

- 🟢 Pick a decision with lasting impact: modularization, a state architecture change, offline-first, a migration, or a platform choice
- 🟢 Explain the context and the quality goals that drove it (`ARCH 2`)
- 🟢 Explain the options and the trade-offs, in plain language
- 🟢 Explain how you introduced it safely: in steps, with checks (`ARCH 16`)
- 🟢 Explain the result and how you knew it worked
- 🟡 How you handled technical debt and rewrites vs refactors
- 🟡 How you documented it and taught the team (`ARCH 14`)
- 🟡 How you kept the design from drifting afterward (`ARCH 17`)
- 🟡 A decision that turned out wrong, and what you changed

## 7. Failure Stories & Learning

- 🟢 Choose a real failure with real consequences, not a hidden strength
- 🟢 Own it. Say what **you** did or missed
- 🟢 Explain how you found out, and how you reacted
- 🟢 Explain what you did to fix it and to limit the damage
- 🟢 Explain what changed afterward: a process, a check, or a habit
- 🟢 Keep the tone calm, honest, and free of blame
- 🟡 A mistake in code review, estimation, communication, or judgment
- 🟡 A project that missed its goal, and what you learned
- 🟡 Feedback you received, and how you acted on it
- 🟡 Common mistakes: blaming others, a fake failure, and no lesson

## 8. Production Incidents

- 🟢 Tell it in order: detect, assess, stop the harm, find the cause, fix, and prevent
- 🟢 Show calm, clear communication during the incident
- 🟢 Show your role: what you personally decided and did
- 🟢 Separate the quick fix from the real root cause
- 🟢 Show the follow-up: a blameless review, actions, and owners
- 🟢 Show the impact: users affected, duration, and cost
- 🟡 Mobile examples: a crash spike after a release, a backend change that broke old app versions, a push notification failure, a bad remote config
- 🟡 Mobile constraints: you cannot instantly roll back an app. Use a staged rollout halt, a feature flag, a server-side fix, or a hotfix (`SD 28`, `MOB 15`)
- 🟡 How you improved monitoring, alerts, or testing afterward (`SD 27`)
- 🟡 On-call experience and how you handled stress

# Stage 3: People & Collaboration

## 9. Conflict Resolution & Disagreement

- 🟢 Tell a real disagreement, with a real cost of not resolving it
- 🟢 Show the steps: listen first, understand their view, find the shared goal, use data, and agree on a way forward
- 🟢 Show respect. Attack the problem, not the person
- 🟢 Show "disagree and commit": you argued your view, then supported the decision
- 🟢 Show the result, and the relationship after
- 🟡 Conflicts with product, design, backend, QA, and other engineers
- 🟡 A time you were wrong, and changed your mind
- 🟡 Escalation: when and how to bring in a manager
- 🟡 Giving direct feedback kindly (the SBI model: situation, behavior, impact)
- 🟡 Common mistake: a story where you were simply right and the other person was simply wrong

## 10. Cross-Team Collaboration

- 🟢 Working with backend, design, product, QA, and other mobile teams
- 🟢 Agreeing on an API contract, and handling changes and old app versions (`BE 3`, `SD 30`)
- 🟢 Handling dependencies: raising them early, and tracking them
- 🟢 Influencing without authority: build trust, share context, and make it easy to say yes
- 🟢 A project that needed several teams to launch, and how you kept everyone aligned
- 🟡 Regular communication: a shared document, a short sync, and clear owners
- 🟡 Disagreeing on priorities between teams, and settling it
- 🟡 Working across time zones or with a remote team
- 🟡 Sharing credit, and making others successful

## 11. Communication & Stakeholder Management

- 🟢 Explain technical topics to non-technical people in simple words
- 🟢 Explain trade-offs in terms of cost, risk, time, and user impact (`ARCH 15`)
- 🟢 Give clear status updates: where we are, what is at risk, and what you need
- 🟢 Set expectations early, and tell bad news early
- 🟢 Say no with reasons and alternatives
- 🟡 Write clear design docs and proposals (`ARCH 14`)
- 🟡 Run useful meetings and reviews
- 🟡 Give and receive feedback
- 🟡 Manage up: keep your manager informed without needing to be asked
- 🟡 Present to leadership: a short story, a recommendation, and a clear ask

# Stage 4: Leadership

## 12. Leadership Without Authority

- 🟢 Leadership is not a title. Show initiative and ownership
- 🟢 A time you saw a problem nobody owned, and drove it to a fix
- 🟢 A time you led a project or a technical direction as a senior engineer
- 🟢 How you got buy-in: a clear proposal, evidence, and small wins
- 🟢 How you raised the quality bar: reviews, standards, tests, or tooling
- 🟢 How you decide and move forward when the answer is unclear
- 🟡 Staff and architect behaviours: setting direction, aligning teams, and removing obstacles (`ARCH 1`)
- 🟡 Staff engineer archetypes: tech lead, architect, solver, and right hand (names)
- 🟡 Acting like an owner: caring about the user and the business, not only your ticket
- 🟡 Leading during a hard or unpopular change

## 13. Mentoring & Growing Others

- 🟢 A time you helped someone grow, with a real outcome
- 🟢 How you coach: ask questions, give context, and let people try
- 🟢 Code review as teaching: explain why, and be kind and clear
- 🟢 Onboarding a new joiner: docs, pairing, and first tasks
- 🟢 Giving feedback that helps: specific, timely, and about behaviour
- 🟡 Delegating: give a goal and context, not only tasks
- 🟡 Mentor vs sponsor: advice vs opening doors
- 🟡 Helping a struggling teammate
- 🟡 Growing the team's skills: talks, docs, and shared practices
- 🟡 What you learned from the people you mentored

## 14. Delivery, Prioritization & Team Health

- 🟢 Prioritizing: impact, effort, risk, and deadlines
- 🟢 Trading off scope, time, and quality, and how you explained the choice
- 🟢 Balancing new features against technical debt (`ARCH 16`)
- 🟢 Estimating with ranges and assumptions, and handling a missed estimate
- 🟢 Unblocking others and removing obstacles
- 🟡 Running or improving a retrospective
- 🟡 Delivery metrics as a team, not to blame individuals (`ARCH 18`)
- 🟡 Protecting the team from constant interruption or burnout
- 🟡 Handling an underperforming teammate with care and clear expectations
- 🟡 Planning a release and the plan for problems (`MOB 15`)

## 15. Hiring & Team Building (New)

- 🟢 How you interview: structured questions, evidence, and fairness
- 🟢 What you look for in a candidate at each level
- 🟡 Writing a clear interview rubric
- 🟡 Reducing bias in hiring
- 🟡 Selling the team to a candidate honestly
- 🟡 Onboarding and setting a new hire up to succeed
- 🟡 Building team culture: trust, learning, and psychological safety
- 🟡 Growing a team's skills mix: mobile, backend, quality, and design

# Stage 5: Self & Career

## 16. Career Story & Motivation

- 🟢 "Tell me about yourself": a 60 to 90 second story from past to present to future
- 🟢 "Why this company and this role?": specific, and connected to your strengths
- 🟢 "Why are you leaving?": positive and honest, without blaming
- 🟢 Your strengths, with examples
- 🟢 Your weaknesses: real, with what you are doing about them
- 🟡 Your career direction: from mobile engineer toward architect or lead, and why
- 🟡 Explaining gaps, moves, or changes in direction
- 🟡 Your working style and what helps you do your best work
- 🟡 What you want to learn next
- 🟡 Where you see yourself in a few years

## 17. Ambiguity, Pressure & Change

- 🟢 A time the goal was unclear, and how you found direction
- 🟢 A time with a tight deadline, and how you kept quality and focus
- 🟢 A time requirements changed late, and how you adapted
- 🟢 A time you had to learn something fast
- 🟢 A time you handled several priorities at once
- 🟡 Staying calm and clear under stress
- 🟡 Pushing back on an unrealistic request with alternatives
- 🟡 Ethical concerns: privacy, dark patterns, and cutting corners on safety
- 🟡 Handling a reorganization or a change in leadership
- 🟡 Looking after yourself: avoiding burnout

# Stage 6: Interview & Career Moves

## 18. Situational & Tough Questions

- 🟢 Behavioral vs situational: "what did you do?" vs "what would you do?"
- 🟢 For situational questions: clarify, state your approach, name the trade-offs, and give an example from real experience
- 🟢 Follow-up probes: expect "why?", "what was your part?", "what would you change?"
- 🟢 When you have no perfect story: use a related one, or say what you would do and why
- 🟡 Trap questions: "biggest weakness", "a time you disagreed with your boss", "a time you failed"
- 🟡 Questions about salary or leaving early, and how to answer honestly
- 🟡 Hypotheticals: "what if the release has a crash and you cannot roll back?"
- 🟡 Staying concise: answer, then stop
- 🟡 Handling a question you did not understand: ask for clarification

## 19. Company Values & Interviewer Types (New)

- 🟢 Research the company: product, users, culture, recent news, and stated values
- 🟢 Map your stories to their values (for example Amazon's Leadership Principles)
- 🟢 Prepare good questions to ask: about the team, quality, decisions, growth, and challenges
- 🟢 Know who you are talking to: hiring manager, peer engineer, product partner, or bar raiser
- 🟡 What each interviewer type listens for
- 🟡 How values-based rounds differ between large companies and startups
- 🟡 Red flags to look for in the company, too: unclear ownership, constant firefighting, and no plan for quality
- 🟡 Follow-up: a short thank-you note
- 🟡 Reading between the lines of the job description

## 20. Offers, Negotiation & Career Moves (New)

- 🟢 Understand the parts of an offer: base pay, bonus, equity, benefits, level, and title
- 🟢 Know your worth: market data and your level
- 🟢 Negotiate calmly and honestly. Ask, explain your reasons, and be ready to walk away
- 🟢 Compare offers on the whole picture: growth, team, scope, and pay
- 🟡 Timing: when to talk numbers, and how to handle multiple processes
- 🟡 Leveling: what a level change means for scope and pay
- 🟡 Notice periods and leaving on good terms
- 🟡 Counter-offers from your current company
- 🟡 Your first 90 days in a new role

## 21. Mock Interviews & Practice Plan

- 🟢 Tell each story out loud with a timer, and record it
- 🟢 Ask a friend or mentor to interview you, and give honest feedback
- 🟢 Listen back: cut filler and background, and strengthen the action and the result
- 🟢 Practise short answers and full answers
- 🟢 Practise with follow-up questions, not only the first answer
- 🟡 Keep your story matrix updated as you practise
- 🟡 Practise in the same setting as the real interview (video call, whiteboard)
- 🟡 Practise the difficult stories most: failure, conflict, and incidents
- 🟡 Plan the day: rest, timing, and a short warm-up

## Skip for Now

- Long theory about management styles and frameworks
- Memorizing scripted answers. Practise stories, not lines
- Company-specific secrets or leaked question lists
- Fancy storytelling tricks. Clear and honest is enough
- Advanced people-management topics (performance plans, compensation planning) unless you are interviewing for a manager role

## How to Proceed

1. Do topics 1 to 3 first. Collect your stories before anything else.
2. Shape your stories one at a time, in STAR form, with a result and a lesson.
3. Cover the core stories (topics 4 to 8) fully. They come up in almost every senior interview.
4. Add the people and leadership stories (topics 9 to 13) from your real experience. Write down at least one for each.
5. Prepare your career story (topic 16) and your company research (topic 19) for each interview.
6. Practise out loud with a timer, and record yourself. Do topic 21 more than you think you need.
7. For each story: the situation in one line, your actions in order, the result with a number, and the lesson.
